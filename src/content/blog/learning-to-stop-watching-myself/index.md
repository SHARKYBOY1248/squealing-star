A few weeks ago, I started doing rejection therapy. The idea is simple: deliberately put yourself in situations where someone might reject you, until rejection starts feeling less scary.

One of my challenges was to walk into a coffee shop and ask the manager if I could be a greeter at the door. If they said yes, I'd have to stand there, smile, and greet customers as they walked in.

Sounds pretty ridiculous, but that was the point.

I remember trying to do this challenge alone on two separate days.

The first time, I'd spent most of the day at home. I walked over to a Starbucks, but couldn't even imagine myself going through with it. I ended up walking right past and going home.

The second time, I tried something different. I spent the day outside, studying at a café, grabbing lunch, and going to the gym. I figured being out and about would make the challenge easier.

It didn't.

I made it to Starbucks, but ended up standing outside for what felt like forever, imagining how awkward and embarrassing it would be to stand at the entrance greeting strangers. Eventually, I gave up and went home.

The funny thing was, **I wasn't even that afraid of the manager rejecting me. I was more afraid they'd say yes.** Then I'd actually have to stand there greeting people, looking completely ridiculous.

But later on another day, after studying and hanging out with some friends for a few hours, we split up to do our challenges individually. And suddenly, approaching the manager felt so much easier.

I actually went through with it this time. Thankfully, the manager said no, so I didn't have to stand there awkwardly greeting people.

**The challenge hadn't changed. My friends weren't even standing next to me. So what had changed?**

And it wasn't just this one challenge. I started noticing a few broader patterns.

Once I'd worked up the courage to attempt one challenge, the next one felt easier. It was as though I'd already crossed some invisible barrier.

But spending an entire day alone seemed to have the opposite effect. Even if I spent the day outside eating at restaurants, studying at cafés, and going to the gym, I found myself becoming progressively less confident around strangers.

I wasn't isolated in my bedroom. I was surrounded by people.

**So why did approaching strangers become harder the longer I went without meaningful social interaction?**

I started wondering whether my difficulty wasn't just about rejection itself, but something else entirely.

## A Day Inside My Own Head

I started thinking back to one particular day when I'd spent almost the entire day alone.

I woke up, did some reading and studying, and eventually decided to get out of the house. I grabbed lunch at Souvla, went to a café to study some more, and later headed to the gym.

Nothing particularly unusual. Honestly, a pretty productive day.

I was surrounded by people for most of it. I even had the occasional interaction with the workers at Souvla or the café, but these were mostly automatic conversations. Ordering food, saying thank you, the usual stuff. Nothing that required much thought.

But as I started paying closer attention to how I'd spent the day, I realized something interesting.

**I was spending an unusual amount of time thinking about myself.**

When I was walking outside alone, I'd sometimes catch myself thinking about how I looked, how confidently I was walking, or how I was carrying myself.

At the café, I was unusually aware of the fact that I was sitting alone, how I was positioned, and what I was doing. At one point, I was watching a video on GPU internals and started wondering whether the person behind me thought I was a classic SF tech bro, with all the stereotypes that come with it.

At the gym, it was more of the same. Between sets, I'd stare at myself in the mirror, check out my physique, or just try to look cool. Sometimes I'd do a few random exercises and leave, then catch myself wondering whether someone thought I was a beginner who had no idea what he was doing.

Sometimes I'd even consciously tell myself, "Who cares what anyone thinks? I'm just gonna do my own thing."

But looking back, even when I was telling myself not to care, **I was still spending mental energy thinking about how other people perceived me.** I was still making their opinions the focus of my attention, just in a different way.

Other times, I'd pick up some heavy dumbbells and imagine the strangers next to me with lighter weights thinking, "Damn, hes strong..."

And throughout the day, I'd occasionally think about the rejection therapy challenge I was supposed to complete. I'd imagine approaching someone, feel hesitant, and put it off.

The longer the day went on, the more I found myself checking how confident or socially comfortable I felt.

Looking back, I noticed a common thread.

**I wasn't just experiencing the world around me. I was constantly imagining how I appeared to the people in it.**

Sometimes I imagined strangers judging me negatively. Other times, like at the gym, I imagined them admiring me. Either way, I was viewing myself through their eyes, even though they probably weren't paying much attention to me in the first place.

I started wondering whether this constant self-monitoring was why I felt increasingly uncomfortable around strangers as the day went on.

Eventually, I came across a psychological concept that seemed to describe what I was experiencing: **self-focused attention**.

## The Psychology of Watching Yourself

Self-focused attention is a psychological phenomenon where your attention shifts inward, toward your own thoughts, feelings, appearance, and behavior, rather than what's happening around you.

Psychologists have studied its relationship with social anxiety for decades. One influential model, developed by David Clark and Adrian Wells, suggests that when we become concerned about how others perceive us, we can start monitoring ourselves almost as if we're observing ourselves from someone else's perspective.

And the interesting thing is, **we don't necessarily need to be consciously thinking, "What if someone judges me?" for this to happen.**

I think this can create a feedback loop.

I start monitoring how I'm coming across. That makes me more aware of the possibility of being judged. I become more self-conscious, which makes me want to monitor myself even more.

And there's another consequence: **the more attention I'm spending evaluating myself, the less attention I'm giving to the world around me.**

Instead of being curious about the people and things around me, I'm monitoring how comfortable or confident I feel.

This helped me form a hypothesis about my experience with rejection therapy.

Maybe after spending hours alone, I was getting increasingly caught up in this habit of self-monitoring. And by the time I wanted to approach a stranger, I was already so conscious of how I might be perceived that even a silly interaction felt intimidating.

Of course, spending time alone doesn't automatically make someone self-conscious, and I can't say for certain that this explains my experience.

**But if self-focused attention was making these challenges harder, why did spending time with friends seem to make it disappear?**

## Why Being With Friends Changes Everything

Initially, I thought the answer was pretty simple. When I'm doing something ridiculous with friends, the social risk feels smaller because it's a shared experience. Even if I embarrass myself, I can go back and laugh about it with them afterward.

And I still think that's part of it.

But it didn't fully explain why I felt so much more confident even after we'd separated to do our challenges individually. Or why I generally felt more comfortable around strangers after spending time with friends, even when I wasn't doing rejection therapy at all.

When I'm hanging out with people I'm comfortable around, I'm not thinking about how I'm standing, how I'm walking, or whether I look confident.

I'm too busy having fun.

I'm listening to conversations, making jokes, laughing, and reacting to whatever's happening around me. My attention is naturally directed outward, toward the people I'm with and the experience we're sharing.

**I'm no longer observing myself. I'm participating.**

And I think that's the key difference.

What's especially interesting is that this feeling seems to carry over even after my friends leave.

It's almost as if I've built up some kind of social momentum. I've spent the last hour being spontaneous, engaged, and comfortable around people. So when I approach a stranger afterward, it feels like a natural continuation of what I've already been doing.

Compare that to spending the entire day alone, monitoring how I look and how others might perceive me. Suddenly, approaching someone requires me to go from observing myself to actively engaging with another person.

That transition feels much harder.

I think something similar might explain why completing one rejection therapy challenge makes the next one easier. After the first interaction, I've already shifted some of my attention away from myself and toward the world around me.

Of course, there are probably other factors involved. Being around friends provides social support, and successfully attempting a challenge can make the next one feel less intimidating. I can't know exactly how much each factor contributes.

But this explanation made me realize something important.

**Maybe the reason I'm so confident around friends isn't that I care less about being judged. It's that I'm barely thinking about being judged in the first place.**

And that changes how I want to approach rejection therapy.

Because ultimately, I don't want to need my friends around to feel this way. I want to be able to spend an entire day alone, explore a new city, meet strangers, and do spontaneous things without constantly worrying about how I'm being perceived.

## Learning to Stop Watching Myself

When I first started rejection therapy, my goal was simple: become less afraid of rejection.

But now I realize there's something else I want to work on.

**I don't just want to become less afraid of rejection. I want to spend less time thinking about myself and more time engaging with the world around me.**

One of the things I love about traveling is the possibility of spontaneous experiences. I want to be able to explore a city alone, strike up conversations with strangers, meet interesting people, and do ridiculous things simply because I feel like it.

I don't want to need a group of friends or a few drinks to feel comfortable doing those things (although of course those would be even more fun).

So how do I get there?

I think the first step is simply becoming more aware of when I'm monitoring myself. Catching those moments when I'm wondering how confidently I'm walking, how I'm sitting, or what a stranger might think about what I'm doing.

Instead of trying to convince myself that nobody is judging me, I want to practice redirecting my attention outward. Toward my surroundings, the things I'm interested in, and the people around me.

And importantly, I don't want to immediately check whether I feel more confident afterward. That would just be another form of self-monitoring.

I also want to keep doing rejection therapy, but with a slightly different purpose.

Rather than just collecting rejections or trying to prove that I can do increasingly embarrassing things, I want to practice acting spontaneously even when I feel self-conscious.

That means initiating conversations, expressing myself without overthinking, and getting comfortable with the possibility that someone might think I'm weird.

It also means practicing these things after spending hours alone, when I don't already have the social momentum that comes from hanging out with friends.

Of course, I don't expect to eliminate self-consciousness entirely. And I don't think that's even the right goal. Being aware of how we come across to other people is a normal and sometimes useful part of social interaction.

What I want to change is how much control that awareness has over my behavior.

I'm still figuring all of this out. I don't know how much of my experience is explained by self-focused attention, or whether these exercises will make the difference I'm hoping for.

But I feel like I have a much clearer idea of what I'm trying to work on.

**Maybe confidence isn't always about learning to care less about what other people think. Sometimes it's about becoming so engaged in what you're doing that you stop thinking about yourself in the first place.**
